# DispatchDesk: A dispatch copilot for dark store managers

**Working-backwards narrative memo — draft for team review**  
**Customer:** Karthik Reddy, dark store manager, Koramangala, Bengaluru  
**Planning horizon:** Four-week prototype; Week 1 is the current build phase  
**Source of scope:** [DispatchDesk requirements](../requirements.md) and [four-week task plan](../tasks.md)

## The decision

We propose building DispatchDesk, a manager-controlled assistant that explains why a store's ten-minute delivery promise is slipping and presents safe, evidence-based options for what to do next. It will combine the store's simulated dispatch data with written operating policy. It will remember the manager's operating preferences across shifts. It will show where a figure came from, when live data was last checked, and which policy supports a recommendation. It will prepare operational actions as drafts for the manager to approve; it will not quietly dispatch riders, change a service radius, activate incentives, or send messages.

This is a four-week prototype for one store persona, not an attempt to replace dispatch software. The first week should prove that a manager can ask a question in a Gradio chat and receive a useful explanation grounded in retrieved SOP and playbook passages. At that stage, the assistant has no live dispatch tools. It must therefore avoid claiming to know what is happening in the queue right now. Later weeks add simulated live tools, persistent preferences, formal guardrail checks, observability, and evaluation. The product earns the right to give more specific guidance only as those sources of evidence become available.

We should proceed because the manager's hard problem is not a lack of dashboards. It is the gap between seeing a late-delivery metric and understanding the cause quickly enough to make a safe decision. The prototype can test whether a conversational interface closes that gap while preserving the manager's control.

## The customer and the problem

Karthik manages a dark store in Koramangala with roughly 600 orders a day, about 2,500 SKUs, nine pickers, and around twenty riders per shift. The period between 7 and 10 p.m. is already demanding; rain makes it harder. A single SLA number can tell him that performance has deteriorated, but it cannot tell him whether pick-pack time, packed-order rider wait, or ride time is driving the change. It does not connect that diagnosis to batching rules, rider breaks, weather guidance, or the store's own operating preferences. Karthik must make these connections while orders are aging and multiple screens demand attention.

Today, a manager facing a backlog may ask several separate questions. How many packed orders are waiting? Which riders are actually available, returning, or due for a break? Did yesterday's comparable peak fail because riders were scarce or because pick-pack slowed? Can two orders be batched without violating zone, detour, item, or cold-chain rules? What should customers be told if rain makes a ten-minute promise unrealistic? The answer to each question may live in a different dashboard, spreadsheet, or policy document. Under time pressure, the manager can default to habit or a generic “go faster” response. That can damage both service quality and rider safety.

The requirements describe a prior rider near-miss and a labor complaint following pressure to hit the SLA at any cost. This is central to the product decision. A dispatch assistant that improves apparent speed by encouraging speeding, missed breaks, overlong shifts, or unfair penalties would fail even if its delivery metric improved. Karthik wants help making better calls, including a clear refusal when a proposed shortcut conflicts with policy. He does not want to hand operational authority to a bot.

## The proposed customer experience

Karthik opens DispatchDesk and asks, “Orders are backing up right now. What is going on, and what should I do first?” In the full prototype, the assistant fetches a fresh simulated queue and rider snapshot, shows its “as of” time, and retrieves the delay-diagnosis guidance. It separates observations from interpretation: which orders are packed, how old the oldest order is, what riders are available or returning, and whether the bottleneck appears to be picking, rider wait, or travel. It then orders a short set of proposals, with the evidence and policy behind each one. Karthik can inspect a proposal before explicitly approving any simulated action. If the dispatch-status tool is unavailable, the assistant says so and does not treat an old snapshot as current fact.

For a retrospective question, such as why the 8–10 p.m. SLA fell compared with the previous evening, DispatchDesk asks the historical metrics tool for both periods. It compares order volume, riders online, rain flags, and the pick-pack, rider-wait, and ride-time stages. It names the stage that changed most and retrieves the relevant rain or surge playbook. The answer uses figures returned by the tool and makes the comparison inspectable. It does not turn a correlation into a claim that the weather alone caused the delay.

When Karthik asks whether to batch packed orders in heavy rain, the assistant checks the actual order attributes and rider state before suggesting pairs. A suggested batch must satisfy the written rules for zones, detour and item limits, and cold-chain exclusions. Orders left out are named with a reason. Any customer ETA is an estimate derived from available tool data, shown as a range and never described as a promise. If the simulated data lacks the inputs needed for a defensible ETA, the assistant says that it cannot estimate one. It can instead draft a truthful customer message for review.

The experience also needs to remember what Karthik has already decided. If he says that weekend alerts after 7 p.m. should trigger above two pending orders per available rider and that frozen items must never be batched, those preferences should be stored and recalled in a later shift. A new proposal that conflicts with a stored preference should identify the conflict and ask him to resolve it. Memory does not authorize an action; it changes which proposals are appropriate and which conflicts need attention.

Safety is visible in the customer experience, not hidden in a policy file. If Karthik asks for a message telling riders to speed or jump signals, or asks to dock pay for weather-related lateness, DispatchDesk refuses to draft it, cites the rider-safety policy, and offers compliant ways to reduce pressure. If he asks to keep a named rider on until close, the assistant first checks that rider's recorded hours and break status. It will not propose a shift that exceeds the policy limit or skips a mandatory break. It should offer alternatives such as standby capacity, zone balancing, a temporary radius change, or escalation to regional operations, each framed as a proposal requiring approval.

## How the prototype works

The team has selected Python with Flask for the backend, Gradio for the manager interface, PostgreSQL with pgvector for document retrieval, `uv` for package management, and Groq-hosted `openai/gpt-oss-120b` for chat generation. An English embedding model, `BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5`, is the current candidate. We will confirm it by testing whether the required rain-delay question retrieves the correct delay guide and rain playbook among the top three passages. The chosen embedding model and vector dimension should be fixed before the corpus is indexed, then versioned so that ingestion and query embeddings stay compatible.

The retrieval corpus will contain dispatch SOPs, a delay root-cause guide, batching and cold-chain rules, a weather and surge playbook, customer-communication guidance, and rider safety and working-hours policy. Each chunk should retain its source title and section so an answer can point back to the rule it used. Retrieval is for policy and operating guidance. It must not substitute for fresh queue data or become a source of invented order counts, rider hours, or ETAs.

The operational data will be synthetic or lightly simulated. It needs a live queue snapshot, per-order attributes, a rider roster with shift and break information, and at least two comparable evenings of hourly delivery-stage metrics, one affected by rain. The data should contain enough information to demonstrate all six required queries, including an eligible and an ineligible batch, a rider close to a policy limit, and a clear change in a delivery stage across two evenings. Timestamps and stable identifiers are necessary: they let the assistant state what was observed, when, and for whom. The dataset should be internally consistent so the demo does not require the model to infer missing numbers.

In Week 1, the prototype will ingest this policy corpus, retrieve relevant passages, and answer policy or explicitly hypothetical scenario questions in Gradio. It may explain, for example, why rain can increase rider wait and what the playbook says a manager should check. It cannot yet say that rider wait is today's actual cause, or give today's queue depth, because tools are scheduled for Week 2. The Week 1 system prompt should already enforce the prohibitions on invented figures, unsafe rider pressure, and unapproved actions. Week 3 adds explicit guardrail checks around outputs; that later work does not relax the Week 1 behavior requirement.

In Week 2, Flask exposes the simulated live dispatch and historical metrics functions through the selected tool interface, and the agent uses them in the chat. The live function returns an “as of” timestamp. Store preferences become persistent across two separate sessions, and a trace panel shows tool calls and recalled preferences. In Week 3, the team checks every operational proposal against current tool data, corpus rules, and stored preferences, then adds cache behavior appropriate to each source: short-lived or no cache for live status, more durable caching for policies and historical metrics. In Week 4, shared trace IDs, failure and stale-data events, a dashboard, and a repeatable six-query evaluation make the system's behavior reviewable.

## Goals and boundaries

Our first goal is a grounded diagnosis. For each required scenario, the assistant should use the right evidence source: retrieval for policy, live tools for current dispatch state, historical tools for past performance, and persistent memory for store preferences. The second goal is a useful next decision. The manager should see a short, prioritized proposal and enough evidence to decide whether to approve it. The third goal is safe control. The assistant should refuse requests that conflict with rider safety or working-hours policy and should never execute an operational change without explicit approval. A fourth goal is inspectability: sources, timestamps, tool failures, stale data, and guardrail interventions should be visible in the demo.

The prototype does not integrate with a real order-management system, rider app, maps service, payment system, or customer messaging channel. It does not optimize routes or guarantee a ten-minute delivery. A simulated approval demonstrates the control boundary; it does not authorize a real-world dispatch action. The prototype's performance on one synthetic store is not evidence that it will improve production SLA or rider outcomes. We will make those limits clear in the UI and demo script, particularly when showing an ETA estimate or comparing two evenings.

The product also should not hide an evidence gap behind fluent language. When the live tool fails, an old snapshot may be shown only with its timestamp and a warning that it may be stale; it cannot be presented as the current queue. When retrieval finds no relevant rule, the assistant should say that policy support is missing. When a request refers to “that rider” or “the late order” without a clear identifier, it should ask which one Karthik means before making a specific proposal.

## Principal risks and mitigations

The most serious risk is a confident but unsupported operational answer. A language model can make a generic rain-delay explanation sound like a diagnosis of today's store, or turn a rough estimate into an apparent customer promise. We will give current-state figures a single source of truth in the tool output, require an “as of” time, and check responses against the data available for that request. We will test failure cases in which data is absent, old, or inconsistent. ETA language will be constrained to an explicitly labeled estimate supported by tool inputs; where inputs are insufficient, no number will be given.

The second risk is unsafe guidance. Written rider-safety and working-hours rules need enough detail to be enforceable: shift maximums, mandatory breaks, and prohibited pressure or penalties cannot remain vague slogans. The corpus will specify those rules. The simulated rider tool will return hours and break information needed to apply them. The agent will refuse unsafe requests at the prompt level from Week 1 and, later, through explicit guardrail checks before proposals reach the manager. A benign break-status question will be included in testing so the guardrail does not block ordinary management questions.

The third risk is that retrieval returns a nearby but wrong passage, especially when a question combines rain, batching, and cold-chain constraints. We will retain document metadata, inspect the top results for representative questions, and evaluate against the six expected behaviors. A retrieval miss should not be patched with model speculation. The fourth risk is a misleading demo built on inconsistent synthetic data. We will define the dataset schema before writing examples, keep the same snapshot available to the tools and test cases, and record what each scenario is intended to prove. The fifth risk is scope: a four-week plan with short task estimates can invite too many features. We will treat the 34 core tasks as the delivery path and leave simulation, proactive alerts, and broader model comparisons as optional work after the core path is sound.

## What success looks like

For Week 1, success is a fresh teammate being able to follow the README, run the app, ask a rain-delay or policy question in Gradio, and see an explanation tied to the relevant corpus passages. The retrieval test should place the delay-diagnosis and rain-playbook passages in the top three for the required rain question. The chat should avoid current-store figures or an ETA because it has no live tools yet. The 6-pager, PR/FAQ, team record, corpus, dataset, ingestion log, retrieval judgment, and UI evidence should be in the repository as described in the task plan.

For the four-week prototype, the acceptance bar is the six sample queries in the requirements document. Each should run end to end and be compared with its expected behavior, with a saved per-case pass or fail result. In addition, two separate sessions should demonstrate that Karthik's preferences are recalled and applied; the live response should display its data timestamp; and an unsafe-pressure or over-hours request should be refused with a policy-grounded explanation and compliant alternatives. No test should produce an unapproved dispatch action or a fabricated operational figure. These are proposed release criteria for the prototype, not claims about current performance.

We will also track tool-call failure rate, stale-data occurrences, how the assistant responded when data was unavailable, guardrail triggers, and preference-recall accuracy. A baseline evaluation followed by error analysis and a rerun after fixes will show whether the prototype improved during Week 4. Latency and cache-hit measurements should be reported alongside correctness, but a faster answer is not a success if it is stale, unsafe, or unsupported. The final demonstration should let Karthik, or a reviewer playing his role, see both the value of a grounded recommendation and the moments when the assistant appropriately says “I cannot tell yet.”
